# Where to open a coffee shop in New York City

Data as of 2026-11-09. 3,241 coffee shops, 197 neighbourhoods, 428 subway stations.

## Recommendation

### 1. Downtown Brooklyn-DUMBO-Boerum Hill (Brooklyn): 79/100

Downtown Brooklyn‑DUMBO‑Boerum Hill already draws a lot of people—each coffee shop nearby gets about 2,064 weekday subway riders, and the whole area sees over 111,000 subway entries a day, so you’ll have steady foot traffic from commuters and nearby workers who are out from 8 am to 4‑8 pm. With 800 storefronts in the neighborhood and only 43 vacant, there are still a few good spaces left, and the median resident income of roughly $44,300 means locals have decent spending power for a daily cup. All existing cafés have earned an A health‑grade, and the overall market score is 79/100, highlighting strong foot traffic, available locations, and a growing café scene. The main thing to watch is the competition: there are already 53 coffee shops (including 16 chains) and 14 new cafés opened in the past two years, so you’ll need a clear concept or niche to stand out.

### 2. Midtown South-Flatiron-Union Square (Manhattan): 79/100

Midtown South-Flatiron-Union Square scores 79/100. Strong on foot traffic, available storefronts, spending power, growing café scene. 105 coffee shops (51 chain, 54 independent). 2,908 weekday subway riders per coffee shop. Chains here: Starbucks, Dunkin, Gregory's Coffee. Watch out: very high rents likely.

### 3. Midtown-Times Square (Manhattan): 79/100

Midtown‑Times Square already supports 222 coffee shops—both chains and independents—so you’ll be joining a proven, busy café scene where 1,856 weekday subway riders typically pass each shop. With 413,985 subway entries across just ten stations and a median resident income of about $65,900, the area offers strong foot traffic and solid spending power for a new café. There are also plenty of storefront options (2,744 total, 181 vacant), which makes finding a location easier than in many other parts of the city. The main thing to watch is the very high rent that Midtown is known for, so be sure your budget can handle that cost.

### 4. SoHo-Little Italy-Hudson Square (Manhattan): 79/100

SoHo‑Little Italy‑Hudson Square already supports 91 coffee shops—73 independent and 18 chains—yet each shop still draws about 938 weekday subway riders, and the area sees 86,276 subway entries each weekday across just six stations, giving you a strong flow of potential customers. The median resident income of roughly $79,000 and the fact that 94 % of existing cafés have an A grade show that locals have spending power and appreciate quality coffee. With 1,652 storefronts in the neighborhood and 61 of them currently vacant, there’s still space to find a good location, and the café scene has been growing, with 30 new shops opening in the past two years. The main thing to watch is the high level of competition and the likely very high rents, so be sure to secure a lease that fits your budget before you commit.

### 5. Upper East Side-Carnegie Hill (Manhattan): 78/100

Upper East Side‑Carnegie Hill already has a thriving café scene—there are 43 coffee shops serving the area, and every one of them holds an A health‑grade, showing strong local demand and high standards. With about 3,000 subway riders passing by each shop on a typical weekday (over 133,000 entries across the six nearby stations) and a median resident income of roughly $72 k, there’s plenty of foot traffic and spending power to support a new café. In addition, the neighborhood has 1,381 storefronts and only 48 are vacant, meaning you’ll have a good selection of locations but also that the market is fairly tight, so choosing a spot with high visibility and easy access is key. The main thing to watch out for is the level of competition—20 chain cafés and 23 independent shops already operate nearby, so you’ll need a clear concept or niche to stand out.

## Operations Analyst

Well‑run cafés tend to earn the top health grade, with 95 % of all NYC coffee shops achieving an A and the highest rates among chains (98 %) and independents (94 %). They avoid the small but serious risk of closure—only 4 % have ever been shut down—by keeping pest‑related issues under control, since two‑thirds of the 119 inspected closures were linked to pests. The most common serious deficiency that can lead to trouble is inadequate cleaning and sanitising of surfaces, a problem that was observed in 1,864 shops.

- 95% of NYC coffee shops hold an A health grade.
- Chains: 98% A grades; independents: 94%.
- 4% of coffee shops were temporarily closed by the Health Department at least once in the last few years.
- 66% of the 119 coffee shops closed by inspectors had a pest problem (mice, roaches, flies or gaps that let pests in) at that inspection.
- Most common serious problem: surfaces not cleaned and sanitised properly (found at 1,864 coffee shops).
